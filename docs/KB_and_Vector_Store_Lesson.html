Lesson 0The problem we're actually solving

Your warehouse report blends two kinds of knowledge:

KindExampleSourceChanges?
Live facts“1,156 assets, 341 critical, PM ratio 99%”PostgreSQLEvery day
Standing knowledge“ISO 55000: <5% of a fleet should be critical”, “Toyota forklift: service every 500 engine hours”, “Factories Ordinance: hoist inspection every 12 months”Standards, manuals, lawsRarely

A language model (Llama 3.3 via Groq) is great at writing, but it does not reliably know your specific standards, and it must never invent numbers. So the whole game is one sentence:

The one sentence Feed the model the right live facts and the right standing knowledge, then ask it to write.

That “feed the right standing knowledge” part is exactly what the Knowledge Base and Vector Store do. Everything below builds toward that one sentence.

Lesson 1What is a Knowledge Base?

Intuition first. A KB is just curated, trusted notes — like the index cards a good teacher keeps. Not the whole textbook (too much noise), but the distilled, reliable bits.

In your project the KB is a plain Python list of “cards” in app/kb/kb_documents.py. Each card looks like this:

# one KB "card" (a chunk of trusted text)
{
  "id":      "kb_engine_hours",
  "section": "Engine Hours — Primary SHAP Failure Driver",
  "tags":    ["engine", "hours", "shap", "forklift", "overdue"],
  "text":    "Engine hours since last service is the top SHAP failure driver... >500 hours is overdue...",
  "source":  "Toyota 7FG/7FD Repair Manual",   # provenance
}

Lesson 1.1 — A KB is two things, not one

This distinction is the most important idea on this page. Your kb_documents.py holds two shapes of knowledge:

ShapeIn your codeConsumed by
Unstructured text cards (KB_DOCUMENTS)the 26 chunks of ISO / SMRP / OEM / legal prosethe vector store → fed to the LLM
Structured lookup tables (dicts/lists)SHAP_KB_MAP, HEALTH_BAND_KB, BENCHMARKS, OEM_SERVICE_TIERS, STATUTORY_INSPECTION_INTERVALS, FMEA_SEVERITY_BY_TYPEthe annotator → deterministic, no LLM
Teacher's note — why two shapes? Prose is flexible but fuzzy (you search it by meaning). Tables are rigid but exact (you look them up by key). You want exact numbers (a 40% brake-health threshold) to come from a table, never from a model's paraphrase. You want rich explanation to come from prose. Mixing these up is how reports get wrong numbers.
✔ Checkpoint Open kb_documents.py. Find one KB_DOCUMENTS entry (prose) and one dict like BENCHMARKS (table). Those are the two halves of your KB.

Lesson 2Why we can't just keyword-search the KB

Say the report needs the “risk analysis” section. The query is conceptually “what drives failure — brakes, hydraulics, engine hours, coolant?”

A naive approach: keyword match — search the KB text for the word “risk.” Problem: a card about brake health is highly relevant to risk but may never use the word “risk.” Keyword search is literal; it misses meaning.

The core difficulty Humans search by meaning; computers store characters. We need a way to make the computer compare meaning — and that is exactly the gap a vector representation closes.

Lesson 3The big idea: turn text into numbers (vectors)

Intuition. Imagine every document becomes a dot in a giant space. Similar-topic documents sit close together; unrelated ones sit far apart. Place your query as a dot too, and “find relevant docs” becomes “find the nearest dots.” That list of coordinates is a vector; a searchable store of them is a vector store / vector database.

brake card • • hydraulic card \ / "risk drivers?" ●───→ (lands here, near brake / hydraulic) / battery card • • ISO 55000 benchmark card forklift-climate card •

Lesson 3.1 — Two ways to make the vectors

This is the fork in the road, and your project deliberately took the simpler path:

MethodHow it builds the vectorProsCons
TF-IDF (what you use)counts word importance statisticallytiny, instant, no model, already in sklearnunderstands words, not deep meaning
Dense embeddings (pgvector)a neural net maps text → ~1536 numbers of meaningtrue semantic understandingneeds a model, slower, costs

Your kb_vector_store.py docstring says this out loud: “Why TF-IDF instead of dense embeddings: sklearn is already installed… KB is small… no model download.” That's a real engineering trade-off, not a shortcut — and it's the right call at 26 documents.

Teacher's note At small scale, TF-IDF is accurate enough and ~1000× cheaper. Dense embeddings earn their cost when you have thousands of docs or must match paraphrases with no shared words. You even left the door open: Supabase has pgvector installed for the day you upgrade. That's mature thinking — pick the simplest tool that works, leave a migration path.

Lesson 4TF-IDF, explained slowly (the heart of it)

TF-IDF = Term Frequency × Inverse Document Frequency. Two ideas multiplied.

Term Frequency (TF): how often a word appears in this document. “brake” appearing 5 times in the brake card → that card is about brakes.

Inverse Document Frequency (IDF): how rare the word is across all cards. “the” appears everywhere → useless → low weight. “hydraulic” appears in few cards → very informative → high weight.

The intuition in one line A word matters if it is frequent here but rare elsewhere — that's a word that characterizes this document.

Lesson 4.1 — Your exact configuration

In kb_vector_store.py you build the vectorizer like this — and every setting is a deliberate lesson:

TfidfVectorizer(
    strip_accents="unicode",   # "Colombo" == "Cólombo"
    lowercase=True,            # "Brake" == "brake"
    stop_words="english",      # drop "the/and/of" — pure noise
    ngram_range=(1, 2),        # index single words AND word-pairs
    max_features=2000,         # keep the 2000 most useful terms
    sublinear_tf=True,         # 10 mentions ≠ 10× important (use log)
)
self._matrix = self._vectorizer.fit_transform(self._texts)

The two clever ones, decoded:

  • ngram_range=(1,2) — also index pairs of words (“engine hours”, “brake health”) as single features. “engine hours” together means far more than “engine” + “hours” apart. Bigrams capture little phrases.
  • sublinear_tf=True — uses 1 + log(count) instead of raw count. A word said 20 times isn't 20× more important than once. Log-scaling tames that and stops long documents from dominating.

That last line, fit_transform, is the moment your 26 text cards become a matrix of numbers (rows = documents, columns = the 2000 terms). That matrix is your vector store. It is built once, in memory, when the app starts.

✔ Checkpoint You now understand that “building the vector store” = “running TF-IDF over the KB text to get one number-vector per card.”

Lesson 5Measuring “nearness”: cosine similarity

We have document-vectors and a query-vector. How do we measure close? Not straight-line distance (a long doc and a short one on the same topic would look “far”). Instead: cosine similarity — the angle between two vectors.

AngleCosineMeaning
0° (same direction)1.0identical topic
90° (perpendicular)0unrelated
Why angle, not length? Length reflects how much text; angle reflects what it's about. We care about topic, so we compare direction. Two arrows pointing the same way are “about the same thing,” whether one is long or short.

In your retrieve():

q_vec  = self._vectorizer.transform([query])        # query → vector (same 2000-term space)
scores = cosine_similarity(q_vec, self._matrix)[0]  # angle vs every doc
top_idx = np.argsort(scores)[::-1][:top_k]          # sort, take the best k
return [ ... for i in top_idx if scores[i] >= min_score ] # drop weak matches

Three small steps, each a lesson:

  1. transform (not fit_transform!) — the query is placed into the existing space. You never re-learn the vocabulary for a query.
  2. argsort[::-1][:top_k] — sort ascending, reverse, take the top top_k (default 4): “give me the 4 nearest cards.”
  3. min_score=0.05 — a quality floor. If even the best match is basically unrelated, return nothing rather than garbage.
⚠ Common beginner bug Re-fitting the vectorizer on the query (fit_transform([query])). That builds a brand-new vocabulary from one sentence, so the query and documents no longer live in the same space and every score breaks. Always transform the query.

Lesson 6Two retrieval styles you built

Your store exposes two doors — knowing which door, when is a genuine skill:

MethodHow it finds docsUse when
retrieve(query)semantic — cosine similarity on meaningopen-ended (“what about failure risk?”)
retrieve_by_tags(tags)deterministic — exact tag overlapyou know exactly the category you want

retrieve_by_tags is the “I want the brake card, no surprises” door — it just counts tag matches. retrieve is the “find me whatever is about this” door. A good system keeps both: fuzzy search for discovery, exact lookup for control.

Lesson 7RAG: how the KB actually reaches the report

Now we assemble everything. The pattern is RAG — Retrieval-Augmented Generation. Read it backwards: we Generate text, Augmented by knowledge we Retrieved. The LLM doesn't work from memory; it works from notes we hand it.

The full pipeline in app/agents/report_agents.py, as a story:

Step 1  build_warehouse_context(db)            # live PostgreSQL facts (the "what is")
Step 2  get_kb_store().build_full_kb_context() # standing knowledge (the "what should be")
Step 3  KB Annotator (deterministic)           # exact threshold/band/FMEA tables, NO LLM
Step 4  LLM call: data + KB → JSON report
Step 5  _guard_narrative()                     # fact-check the model's prose

Lesson 7.1 — A subtle, important choice you made

For the report, you don't use retrieve() at all — you call build_full_kb_context(), which dumps all 26 cards:

kb_full_context = kb_store.build_full_kb_context()   # the WHOLE KB

Why abandon clever retrieval here? Because 26 curated cards ≈ 5,000 tokens, and Llama 3.3 has a 128,000-token window. The whole KB fits with room to spare — so why risk top-k retrieval dropping a relevant card? Inject everything; let the model see the full picture.

The lesson behind the lesson Retrieval exists to handle scale you can't fit. When everything fits, retrieval is premature optimization. You correctly use full-context for the report (completeness matters) and keep retrieve() for the chatbot (route_request), where a focused answer beats a data dump. Right tool, right place.

Lesson 8The other half: deterministic annotation

This is where your design is genuinely sophisticated — and the lesson most tutorials skip.

The LLM writes prose. But the exact numbers — “brake health <40% → ground the asset,” “FMEA criticality = severity × occurrence” — must be computed, not narrated. So app/kb/kb_annotator.py takes the live data and the structured KB tables and produces facts with zero LLM involvement:

annotate_shap_features(...)   # look up each SHAP driver's KB threshold
annotate_health_bands(...)    # attach the KB interpretation to each band
rank_fmea_criticality(...)    # severity × occurrence, computed in Python
compute_benchmark_alerts(...) # compare live % against ISO/SMRP benchmarks
The golden rule you're enforcing Words from the model; numbers from the data. The model never decides a threshold — it only describes one the annotator already fixed. This is why your reports can be defended in a viva: every number traces to a query or a KB constant, not a guess.

And _guard_narrative() (Lesson 7, Step 5) is the seatbelt: even after all that, it re-reads the model's paragraph and corrects it if it claims “costs rising” when the data says falling. Trust, but verify.

Lesson 9The pgvector future (where this grows)

Your docstrings mention Supabase has pgvector installed. Here's the upgrade path, so you can speak to it:

StageHow vectors are made & storedHow you search
TodayTF-IDF vectors live in memory (sklearn matrix), rebuilt at startup. Perfect for 26 static cards.cosine_similarity() in Python
TomorrowEach card → an embedding model → a dense vector, stored in a Postgres vector column via pgvector.ORDER BY embedding <=> q LIMIT k (<=> = cosine distance, in the database)

Same three concepts — vectorize → store → nearest-neighbour search — just industrial-strength. The mental model you learned today doesn't change; only the engine does. That's why concepts beat tools.

BonusWorked example: TF-IDF + cosine by hand

Let's make it concrete with 3 tiny “cards” and one query, so you see the numbers form.

# Tiny KB (imagine these are the full card texts)
D1 = "brake health critical ground asset"
D2 = "engine hours service overdue forklift"
D3 = "battery health swap electric forklift"

Query = "forklift overdue for service"

Step 1 — vocabulary (unique informative words across all cards): brake, health, critical, ground, asset, engine, hours, service, overdue, forklift, battery, swap, electric.

Step 2 — IDF (rarity). A word in fewer cards scores higher. “forklift” is in D2 & D3 (common → lower IDF); “overdue” and “service” are only in D2 (rare → higher IDF).

Step 3 — vectorize the query. The query shares forklift, overdue, service — all present in D2. It shares nothing meaningful with D1, and only the (common, low-weight) forklift with D3.

Step 4 — cosine scores (illustrative):

CardShared informative terms with queryCosine ≈Result
D2forklift, overdue, service0.71✅ returned (top match)
D3forklift (low weight only)0.12returned if k allows
D1—0.00❌ below min_score → dropped
What you just saw The engine-hours / service card (D2) won — without the query ever using its exact words like “engine” or “hours.” It won because the rare, characterizing words (“overdue”, “service”) lined up. That's meaning-by-statistics, and that's the whole trick.

RecapThe cards to keep

  1. KB = curated trusted notes, in two shapes: prose cards (search by meaning) and lookup tables (look up by key).
  2. Vectors turn meaning into geometry — similar text → nearby points.
  3. TF-IDF weights words that are frequent here but rare elsewhere; bigrams, sublinear TF and stop-words all sharpen that signal.
  4. Cosine similarity compares direction (topic), not length (size).
  5. transform the query into the existing space, sort, apply a min_score floor — say nothing rather than mislead.
  6. RAG = hand the model retrieved notes instead of trusting its memory.
  7. Full-context beats retrieval when everything fits — retrieval is for scale you can't fit.
  8. Words from the model, numbers from the data — deterministic annotation + a narrative guard keep the report honest.
  9. pgvector is the same idea at scale; the concept is portable.
